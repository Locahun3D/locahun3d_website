Robot Development in a 3DGS City
with Isaac Sim 6.0:
RTX LiDAR, Sun Shadows, Autonomous Navigation

This is Nakamura from LOCAHUN 3D. On top of the two-layer environment from last time (untouched 3DGS + raw scanner mesh), this article runs the three things robot development actually needs in NVIDIA Isaac Sim 6.0: a real sensor (RTX LiDAR) sweeping the city, shadows that move with the time of day (a fixed-view offline composite), and a map-based autonomous drive to a goal. The robot is Isaac's stock Nova Carter; the stage is our scan of Tokyo's Shibuya scramble crossing. Videos first, then the traps you will hit and an honest list of what does not work yet.

What you getStarting from last article's two-layer USD (3DGS + invisible collider), you end with three working pieces: (1) a stock Isaac robot driving with LiDAR point-cloud capture, (2) time-of-day shadows using the raw scanner mesh as caster, (3) a map generated from the collider and a robot following a path to a goal. On the simulation side everything is bundled with Isaac Sim 6.0.1 — no extra installs, no ROS 2.

01 Setup, and which surface each sensor sees

The environment is unchanged from last time: appearance = untouched 3DGS, physics = invisible collider made of the raw scanner mesh + ground patches. Isaac Sim 6.0 organises 3DGS import around USD ParticleField, has a new RTX LiDAR API, and now documents a ROS 2 setup for Windows (not tested here).

First, which surface each sensor sees — three things we measured. (1) RTX LiDAR does hit the 3DGS itself: with only the 3DGS shown, ground and buildings return, and with everything hidden the returns drop to zero. This is our measurement on Isaac Sim 6.0.1; NVIDIA's forum answer for 6.0.0 said splats were invisible to the LiDAR, so it is not guaranteed behaviour — re-check on any version change, and ship mesh returns (3) as well. (2) It does not hit invisible prims, so the invisible collider is not seen and the LiDAR returns sit a few centimetres off the collision surface (small for a robot, and actually convenient because camera and LiDAR see the same surface). (3) If you want returns from the mesh instead, give a mesh a cut-out transparent material: on our 6.0.1 the camera did not see it but the LiDAR still hit it (other transparency settings also hide it from the LiDAR, so acceptance-test the settings you use). Shadows are the exception: a shadow caster is always visible to the camera, so Demo 2 is a composite.

Test environmentIsaac Sim 6.0.1 on Windows 11 with a GeForce RTX 5090; the loaded environment uses well over ten gigabytes of VRAM. Z-up, metres, world origin at the scanner's local origin (no geo-reference). Not tested on Linux. The videos play at about 2× simulation time; all speeds and times in the text are sim time. Some of the extensions used are marked experimental — pin your version.

02 Demo 1: Nova Carter + RTX LiDAR sweeping the city

Four steps. (1) Robot: load the stock Nova Carter and drive it with the bundled differential controller. (2) Sensor: parent a bundled RTX LiDAR (a real-model configuration) to the chassis and read the point cloud every frame. (3) Control: a simple heading controller toward a point a few metres ahead. (4) Drawing: transform the points to world coordinates and draw them in the viewport.

Traps
  • For mesh returns, place a mesh of the collider's shape with a cut-out transparent material: the camera does not see it, the LiDAR does, and both are still captured in one drive.
  • Transforming the lidar points to world put the ground one sensor-mount height too low. Adding the mount offset fixes it.
  • Do not overwrite the wheel drive settings yourself. The asset defaults are more stable.
  • Above walking pace the casters start oscillating and the robot spins on the raw scan road. This is a simulator-side effect, not the real robot, so gains or stability tuned here do not transfer.
  • Reloading the robot at the same path drops its body. Re-open the stage per take. Repeated re-opens can also turn the 3DGS black; restart Isaac when that happens.
  • No motion distortion in the point cloud (default setting). Enable it separately for SLAM tests.

03 Demo 2: raw-mesh shadows on the splats (sun time-lapse)

As the Omniverse docs state, in 6.0 mesh shadows do land on the splats (the robot's shadow in Demo 1 is exactly that). But splat colour is baked, so adding lights does not brighten anything and colour temperature does not change. Shadows yes, relighting no — that is the accurate 6.0 statement.

To cast building and lamp shadows you use the raw mesh as caster, but it cannot be hidden from the camera. So we composite three renders: the splats alone, a white-painted mesh under the sun with shadows, and the same without shadows. The ratio of the last two isolates the shadows, which are then multiplied onto the splat image.

LimitsThe shadow mask comes from the mesh and lands on the splats, so shadows are displaced by the offset between the two surfaces (centimetres nearby, over a metre in the far field): fine for near road shadows, wrong against distant building outlines. It is a fixed-camera offline composite and cannot be applied to a moving robot camera as is. Received shadows are blurry by design and stack on the shadows baked into the scan, so overcast scans look most natural. Night scenes or lit neon are not possible in 6.0.

04 Demo 3: build a map and drive to a goal

The map comes from Isaac's bundled occupancy-map generator in seconds. To keep small road bumps from becoming obstacles it only looks at a band from just above the ground up to the robot's height. The path is a shortest path on the map inflated by the robot's size, thinned to a few waypoints the robot follows.

Traps
  • With our frame conventions the occupancy data came out mirrored left-right (in x). Without the flip the planner "avoids empty road and drives into obstacles" (our first goal landed on top of the police box). We have not isolated whether this is the generator's convention or our transform, so always validate against a check map of known shape before use.
  • If the band's top is below the robot's height, anything that protrudes only in that gap (awnings, sign arms) is not mapped. We checked that nothing in the driven area does; for general use set the top above the robot.
  • Inflating by only the robot's circumscribed radius leaves no safety margin — use more in tight spaces.
  • Kerbs are not obstacles with this band. Nova Carter's front casters cannot climb them, so lower the band's bottom if a route may mount the pavement.

On ROS 2 / Nav2: the Isaac Sim 6.0 docs describe a Windows setup, but this article uses its own planner and no ROS. The grid exports to an image, but the Nav2 map format has its own conventions for origin and colours, and given the x-flip above the conversion is not trivial. We have not driven it under Nav2 yet.

05 Follow-up: giving the 3DGS semantic labels

The biggest hole in this environment was "no segmentation ground truth". So we tried putting the labels on the 3DGS itself (per point) and rendering a class-coloured duplicate of the same points. 3DGS renders its baked colours as-is, so if the duplicate's colours are class colours you get a label image from the same camera and the same points as the RGB, which aligns better than going through the mesh (boundary error from semi-transparent compositing, prediction mistakes and fill-in remains).

How: render the street from many robot-height viewpoints, let a public 2D segmentation model predict classes, aggregate those predictions per 3DGS point by voting while checking with the collider whether the point was actually visible from each view, fill points that were never seen from their neighbours, and write out a duplicate with class colours (the source data stays untouched; only an override layer is added).

Result: on new viewpoints, road, buildings and vegetation come out right; thin things like poles are weak and sidewalk vs road is unstable. Two 2D models disagree with each other about as much, so aggregating in 3D loses little.

These are pseudo-labels, not ground truth (limits)
  • The label source is an AI prediction, not human annotation. The agreement above measures how well we match models of the same kind, not accuracy. 2D mistakes — a street lamp read as a traffic light — propagate into 3D.
  • Sidewalk/road confusion, thin objects and boundaries, and scanned-in pedestrians are not usable. Sky is black (needs an ignore treatment). Filled regions bleed at class boundaries. There are no instance IDs.
  • The use case stops at novel-view generation with pseudo-labels / weakly supervised augmentation. To use them as training ground truth, a small human-labelled set comes first.

06 What is still missing for a production simulator

"It ran" and "it is usable" are different things, so here is the honest list.

  • The camera and the LiDAR/collision see different surfaces. RGB and LiDAR come from the splats, collision from the raw mesh, a few centimetres apart on the road and over a metre in the far field. Camera and LiDAR seeing the same surface is convenient for fusion; just remember that the surface the LiDAR returns is not the surface the robot collides with.
  • Semantics stop at pseudo-labels (section 05): no human-GT check and no instance IDs (confidence-based selection is available).
  • Ghosts and pedestrians. People caught in the scan remain as static obstacles; nothing moves.
  • Sensor realism. No motion distortion, default noise for intensity, IMU and camera; no validation against a real-robot log at the same place.
  • ROS 2 / Nav2 / Linux. All untested; no TF or robot description since ROS is not used. Capture ran well below real time; human-in-the-loop use needs optimisation.
  • Relighting is shadows only. No night, no rain. Speed tops out at walking pace (faster or vehicle-scale belongs to last article's PhysX Vehicle).

07 Summary and deliverables

  • Sensors: RTX LiDAR hits the 3DGS (not invisible colliders). For mesh returns, use a cut-out transparent mesh. Add the mount height when moving points to world.
  • Shadows: in 6.0 mesh shadows land on the 3DGS. The caster cannot be hidden, so composite three renders. No relighting.
  • Map and path: the bundled occupancy generator works in seconds. Check the left-right flip against a ground-truth map.
  • Robot: let the bundled controller drive Nova Carter, stay at walking pace, reopen the stage per take.
  • Semantics: labels on the 3DGS itself render pixel-aligned with the RGB, but they are pseudo-labels, not ground truth.
  • Before you rely on it: the camera and LiDAR/collision see different surfaces; no real-robot validation, no Nav2 drive, no Linux run. Its scope is proof-of-concept work — USD conversion, sensor capture, path demos; it is not, as is, a basis for control or perception performance evaluation or sim-to-real claims.

When we hand over such an environment the deliverable is the two-layer USD (3DGS reference + invisible collider) / the sensor mesh / the occupancy grid (image + origin & resolution) / spawn points and a driven-verified lane / the reproduction scripts, plus the verified versions with a spec sheet for frame and units / an acceptance-test script with expected output / the licence scope of the scan data. The last item matters: the raw Shibuya scan contains pedestrians' faces and vehicle plates. Commercial delivery for AI training includes either masking or a contractual limit on use. The videos in this article use the raw data with no individual masking; the cuts were chosen so people appear only far away at low resolution — we will replace any cut on request.
