Cleaning Up 3DGS Noise
with LiDAR

This is Nakamura from LOCAHUN 3D. Put a 3DGS scan into a robotics simulator and two problems show up immediately: stray specks floating in mid-air that pollute what the robot's camera sees, and holes in the collision mesh that the robot drops straight through. NVIDIA recently published a fix for this, and the method behind it estimates depth with a neural model to constrain the geometry. But what if you already have measured depth from a LiDAR scanner? We built both routes and measured them against each other.

01 Two walls you hit in simulation

3DGS is unbeatable at looking right. But that is the result of optimising to match photographs, not of getting the shape correct. In robotics simulation that distinction turns into concrete failures.

Wall 1: airborne specks pollute the robot's view

3DGS leaves gaussians in places where nothing exists. From certain angles they make visual sense, so they survive training instead of being pruned away.

It is easy to misread what this costs you. 3DGS carries no collision geometry, so a floater will never physically hit the robot. The damage is that it lands in the robot's camera image. If you are training behaviour from camera frames, specks drifting through the view are noise in the training data. And if you derive depth or a mesh from the 3DGS, those specks get picked up as shape.

Wall 2: the robot falls through holes in the collision mesh

Collision needs surfaces, and 3DGS is a cloud of specks with no surfaces. So you reach for the mesh the scanner exports — which brings its own problem. Measured on our scan, 4.86% of the scanner mesh's edges were boundary edges that close onto nothing. In other words, it is full of holes.

The holes are heavily skewed, though. Casting rays straight down to test for floor, 99.90% of the carefully scanned room was closed, while out where coverage thins — corridors and edges — only 22% of the area registered as floor at all. In other words, the holes cluster outside the well-scanned core. Two patches of floor that look identical can be completely different as collision geometry.

Put the robot down where coverage is thin and this happens. The fall does not stop; it keeps accelerating past 38 metres below the floor.

02 NVIDIA's answer, and the assumption inside it

In August 2026 NVIDIA published a solution to exactly this. Using Niantic Spatial's reconstruction pipeline, a single capture emits gaussians and a mesh in one shared coordinate frame — the former is what the robot sees, the latter is what it collides with. The gaussian side of this in Isaac Sim ships as Omniverse NuRec.

The crux is "constraining the geometry with a proprietary depth model." Optimise for appearance alone and you keep the floaters and the holes; depth information pins the shape down. In learning terms this is regularisation with depth as a supervisory signal, and the research lineage is public — DN-Splatter implements the same idea with code you can run.

The assumption we noticedNiantic's case was captured with a 360° camera. With only cameras, estimating depth is the only option available. So what happens when you hold measured depth from a LiDAR scanner? That question is where this went.

03 Two routes

With real LiDAR in hand there are two routes, and you cannot tell which wins without building both.

Intuition says Route B wins: fixing the shape during training is more fundamental than deleting afterwards. We built both and put them on one ruler.

04 Route A: subtract using measured LiDAR

The idea is plain. For every gaussian, compute the distance to the LiDAR point cloud and delete the ones that sit too far away. A speck far from a measured surface has nothing real behind it.

Here is the distribution across the meeting-room scan (936,016 gaussians).

Distance to LiDAR surfaceValueReading
Median6 mmMost gaussians sit right on the measured surface
90th percentile18 mmSurface fuzz stays within a few centimetres
Beyond 10 cm8,406 (0.90%)These are the floaters

One thing became clear here. 3DGS produced by a LiDAR scanner starts with under 1% floaters, because training begins from LiDAR points and so places gaussians near measured surfaces from the outset. That is a different starting position from 3DGS built out of photographs alone.

The image at the top of this article shows the removed gaussians on their own. They are not faint debris but solid artefacts at 0.93 opacity, pooled below the floor and behind walls as ghost shapes.

Rebuild collision from the point cloud

Rather than using the holed scanner mesh, we rebuild surfaces from the LiDAR point cloud with Poisson reconstruction. That method produces closed surfaces, so holes do not appear by construction.

MethodTrianglesHoles (boundary edges)Deviation from LiDARDrop test
Scanner mesh19,3441,444—falls through

Poisson took both prizes: 19× lighter than the voxelised mesh and more accurate. Dropping 56 cubes of 0.1 m across the room, all 56 came to rest on the floor.

The remaining limit, and the RL-side fixA Poisson mesh is watertight, but it cannot invent surfaces where the LiDAR never looked (the far corridor in this scan). Environment generation alone will not close that gap, so you handle it on the learning side: sample spawns and waypoints only inside the observed region, and terminate the episode when the robot leaves it — the same treatment racing RL gives "off track." A hidden safety plane under the floor turns any residual fall into a clean failure signal. Since the 3DGS also degrades outside the scanned region, keeping the policy's operating envelope inside the scan is what you want anyway.

05 Route B: retrain with depth supervision

Now the same principle as NVIDIA, driven by measured LiDAR. One obstacle stood in the way first: training needs to know where each photograph was taken from, and those camera poses were missing from the scanner output.

Recovering the camera poses

The standard tool for recovering poses from photographs is COLMAP, and it failed, registering 6 of 163 images. Indoor scenes full of blank walls are exactly what classical feature extraction struggles with.

Switching to learned features (DISK) and a learned matcher (LightGlue) recovered 126 images. The newer methods simply work better here.

Where it went wrong: the scale would not settleA reconstruction from photographs carries no information about real-world size. We tried to fix the scale by registering against the LiDAR point cloud, but point-cloud registration has a trap: shrinking one cloud until it nests inside the other scores well by the usual metrics, so every run produced a different answer.

The fix looks like a detour and is far more reliable. Render 48 views from the 3DGS that already lives in the LiDAR frame, and treat those as photographs whose positions are known. Matching the real photographs against them recovers each camera position at true scale.

With the recovered poses and LiDAR-derived depth maps in place, we trained DN-Splatter for 30,000 steps. Training completed and the simultaneous mesh export worked.

07 Why it came out this way

Reading this as "DN-Splatter is worse" would be wrong. The cause is the quality of what we fed it.

3DGS training assumes camera poses accurate to well under a centimetre. The poses we recovered were accurate to 13.2 cm at the median. Train on teachers that are 13 cm off and a blurry result is the only possible outcome. We also had just 126 images, against an original 3DGS built by the scanner's own pipeline from every frame with LiDAR initialisation — a badly uneven contest.

So what we measured is not "how good DN-Splatter is" but "retraining from recovered poses cannot beat the original data." With accurate poses from the scanner itself, the conclusion could change.

There is also a more fundamental reason. Route B's mesh is built from depth rendered out of the trained 3DGS, so its quality ceiling is whatever the 3DGS learned. Route A uses the LiDAR measurements directly. Once you think about it, routing measured sensor data through a learned representation can only lose information along the way.

Why Niantic reached for a depth modelThis is where it clicked. They had only a 360° camera, so estimating depth was the only path open to them. Anyone holding measured LiDAR has a shorter and more certain route. The same problem has a different best answer depending on the hardware in your hands.

08 Summary

If you are taking LiDAR-scanned data into robotics simulation, the procedure is this.

  • Remove floaters by distance from the LiDAR — measure each gaussian against the measured surface and delete anything beyond 10 cm. In the meeting room that was 0.90% of them.
  • Build collision from the LiDAR points with Poisson reconstruction — skip the scanner mesh, which is full of holes. You get 94k triangles, zero holes, 2.3 cm accuracy.
  • You do not need to retrain — at least when accurate camera poses are unavailable, post-processing is both faster and better.

Because no retraining is involved, this runs across an entire scan library in minutes. It depends on Python and a point-cloud library, with no GPU training stack to assemble — which matters just as much in production.

The basics of loading 3DGS into Isaac Sim are covered in a separate article, along with how to handle what 3DGS cannot carry on its own, such as collision and semantic labels.
