Turning Shibuya Crossing into
an Environment Robots Can Drive

This is Nakamura from LOCAHUN 3D. We converted a 3DGS scan of Tokyo's Shibuya scramble crossing — 16.5 million gaussians — into a simulation environment where a forklift drives under a physics engine. The post-processing behind it is two steps: delete, then seal, both ruled by measured LiDAR. The recipe was selected by benchmarking six approaches (learned ones included) on a meeting-room scan, and it carries to city-block scale by changing exactly two parameters.

What this article gives youThe inputs are the three standard outputs of a LiDAR-fusion scanner — the 3DGS, the scanner mesh, and the dense LiDAR point cloud (the procedure does not apply to camera-only 3DGS). Two post-processing steps later you hold a two-layer environment: the visible 3DGS stacked on an invisible watertight collider. The collider gives the robot its physics; the 3DGS gives it its camera feed.

01 Two walls you hit in simulation

3DGS is unbeatable at looking right. But that is the result of optimising to match photographs, not of getting the shape correct — and in robotics simulation the difference turns into concrete failures.

Wall 1: airborne specks pollute the robot's view

3DGS leaves gaussians where nothing exists. 3DGS carries no collision, so nothing bumps into them — the damage is that they land in the robot's camera image as training noise.

How high this wall is depends on where you scan. Indoors, the meeting-room scan carried 0.90% floaters; outdoors in Shibuya the count jumped to 1.12 million of 16.5 million gaussians — 6.80%. Glass facades, open sky, and passing crowds all leave specks with nothing real behind them.

Wall 2: the robot falls through holes in the collision mesh

3DGS has no surfaces, so collision comes from the mesh the scanner exports — and measured, the holes (edges that close onto no face) run to 4.86% in the meeting room and 9.01% in Shibuya. They cluster where coverage thins: behind glass, in the shadow of crowds, on road the cameras never faced.

Put the robot down on holed floor and this happens.

02 NVIDIA's answer, and our assumption

In August 2026 NVIDIA published a fix for exactly this: Niantic Spatial's pipeline emits gaussians and a mesh in one coordinate frame from a single capture — appearance and collision respectively. The crux is constraining the geometry with a depth model; DN-Splatter is the public research lineage.

The assumption we noticedNiantic captured with a 360° camera — with only cameras, estimating depth is the only option. We hold measured depth from a LiDAR scanner. Then maybe neither the estimator nor the retraining is needed at all? That question drove everything below.

03 The answer: delete, then seal — live in Shibuya

What won is a two-step post-process with no training whatsoever. It depends on nothing but Python and a point-cloud library, and moving from a room to a city block means changing two numbers: the distance threshold and the voxel size.

Step 1: delete specks far from the LiDAR (floater removal)

Measure each gaussian's distance to a measured surface (the LiDAR cloud, or the scanner mesh sampled densely) and delete anything beyond the threshold. The meeting room used 10 cm; Shibuya used 25 cm for street scale, deleting 1.12 million gaussians (6.80%). Seen from robot eye level, the effect is unmistakable.

Step 2: seal only the holes in the scanner mesh (collision)

Voxelise the scanner mesh, apply morphological closing (dilate–erode) to seal the small holes, and run marching cubes to get a surface back. For Shibuya we cropped 90×90 m around the crossing and processed it at 6 cm voxels with two closing iterations — turning a mesh with 9.01% holes into a fully watertight collider (0% boundary edges, 1.2 million triangles).

Finale: drive a forklift through it

Stack the two layers, open the result in NVIDIA Isaac Sim, and drive a forklift (3.7 m long) under the physics engine.

What this meansA plain rule — do not delete or invent measured data — beat the learned priors. Niantic reached for a depth model because a 360° camera was all they had. Anyone holding measured LiDAR has a shorter, more certain route. The best answer to the same problem changes with the hardware in your hands.
Limits of this benchmarkThe numbers come from two scenes: the meeting room and Shibuya. No surface can be built where the LiDAR never looked, so keep spawns and routes inside the observed region when training. Outdoors adds two caveats. First, pedestrians and temporary fixtures caught mid-scan persist into the collider as real obstacles. Second, real objects the LiDAR cannot reach — billboards high on facades — can be deleted along with the floaters (raising the threshold keeps them, but keeps floaters too). Anything needing millimetre-accurate shape still deserves a CG model.

05 Summary

Taking LiDAR-scanned data into robotics simulation fits in three lines.

  • Remove floaters by distance from measured surfaces — threshold 10 cm indoors, 25 cm at street scale. The amount removed changes by an order of magnitude: 0.90% indoors versus 6.80% outdoors.
  • Build collision by sealing only the holes in the scanner mesh — voxelise, morphological closing, marching cubes. Voxels: 3 cm for a room, 6 cm for a city block. Both come out watertight with 0% holes, desk legs and signal poles intact.
  • No retraining needed — at least when accurate camera poses are unavailable, post-processing is both faster and better.
