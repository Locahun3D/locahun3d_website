Setting Up a 3DGS Simulation
Environment for Isaac Sim

This is Nakamura from LOCAHUN 3D. This is a working guide to turning a LiDAR-fusion 3DGS scan into a simulation environment robots can drive under physics in NVIDIA Isaac Sim. It takes five steps and no retraining. Throughout, a 16.5-million-gaussian scan of Tokyo's Shibuya scramble crossing serves as the running example, with measured numbers and the traps we actually hit.

What this article gives youThe inputs are the three standard outputs of a LiDAR-fusion scanner — the 3DGS, the scanner mesh, and the dense LiDAR point cloud (the procedure does not apply to camera-only 3DGS). Five post-processing steps later you hold a two-layer environment — the visible 3DGS stacked on an invisible collider — ready for robot driving, collision, and sensor tests in Isaac Sim. Dependencies: Python, a point-cloud library, and Isaac Sim.

01 The big picture — why post-processing at all

3DGS is unbeatable at looking right. But that is the result of optimising to match photographs, not of getting the shape correct — and used raw in simulation, two failures follow.

First, floaters: gaussians left where nothing exists land in the robot's camera as training noise. The amount depends heavily on the scene — measured at 0.90% indoors versus 6.80% (1.12 of 16.5 million) in outdoor Shibuya. Second, holes in collision: 3DGS has no surfaces, so collision comes from the scanner mesh, whose boundary-edge holes run to 4.86% indoors and 9.01% in Shibuya. Put a robot on a hole and this happens.

The architecture that fixes both is two layers: a floater-cleaned 3DGS for appearance, an invisible watertight collider for physics. NVIDIA×Niantic's pipeline of the same shape constrains geometry with AI-estimated depth — but that is what you do when a 360° camera is all you have. Holding measured LiDAR depth, you need neither the estimator nor any retraining: the five steps below build the same thing (quantitative comparison against learned methods is in the benchmark section).

02 STEP 1: Load the 3DGS into Isaac Sim

Convert the 3DGS PLY to USD (ParticleField) and load it — the basic conversion workflow is covered in a separate article. For a working setup, defuse two traps first.

  • Align the coordinate frame. A Y-up 3DGS (e.g. from LCC2) converts to Z-up with positions mapped (x, z, -y) — a -90° rotation about X — and orientations left-multiplied by the same rotation's quaternion. When touching USD quaternion arrays from numpy, the memory layout is (x, y, z, w) — imaginary first; assume (w, x, y, z) and every gaussian's orientation breaks, turning the scene to noise.
  • Pin the renderer to RaytracedLighting before opening the stage. Path-tracing modes blow the self-emissive 3DGS out to white, and switching modes after a 10-million-gaussian scene is loaded is a reliable way to crash.
The unit trap when placing robotsReference assets authored in centimetres (Isaac's stock forklift among them) get an automatic 0.01 correction scale from Kit. Rebuild the xform with ClearXformOpOrder() and that correction disappears — enter a 370-metre forklift. After rebuilding, set scale 0.01 explicitly.

03 STEP 2: Delete the floaters

Measure each gaussian's distance to a measured surface (the LiDAR cloud, or the scanner mesh sampled densely) and delete what floats — but not with a single threshold. A flat cutoff also deletes real things the LiDAR simply never reached: high billboards, the far ends of streets. Use bands instead: 25 cm–2 m above the measured surface deletes unconditionally (the haze over the roads), and beyond 2 m only wispy low-opacity gaussians go (measured, the far band's median opacity is 0.17 — clearly separated from solid content). Shibuya loses 0.97 million gaussians (5.88%), keeping ~150k real far-field gaussians a flat threshold would have destroyed.

04 STEP 3: Build a collider that patches only the holes

First, voxelise the scanner mesh, seal the holes with morphological closing (dilate–erode), and run marching cubes — 3 cm voxels for a room, 6 cm for a city block turn a 9.01%-holed mesh into a watertight shell with 0% boundary edges.

Used directly, though, that shell has a melted-wax look that is worse geometry than the raw mesh. So use it only as a patch source: keep the shell triangles that sit more than 5 cm from the raw surface — the parts spanning holes — and overlay them on the untouched raw mesh. Shibuya's composite is 510k raw + 310k patch triangles: raw fidelity, holes backed (vertical ray test over the drivable area shows zero leaks; unobserved building interiors excluded).

05 STEP 4: Smooth the road, remove the ghosts

The STEP 3 collider already serves collision, path-planning, and sensor-occlusion checks — but vehicles need one more pass. Voxel reconstruction leaves a staircase texture on the road measured at bump p95 = 6.0 cm (exactly the voxel pitch), and a suspension-less vehicle transmits all of it. Snapping just the road region onto a smooth field fitted to the measured LiDAR surface brings bumps down to p95 = 1.0–1.6 cm — real-asphalt territory — and aligns the collider with the 3DGS ground (curbs and structures untouched, still watertight).

The same pass removes pedestrians and stopped vehicles caught mid-scan — frozen obstacles. Cluster everything protruding from the road, classify person/cart-sized shapes (0.25–4.2 m wide, under 2.7 m tall) plus floating shards, then rasterise each cluster's actual silhouette into a 10 cm mask and, inside the mask only, flatten the collider and delete the matching gaussians (210 footprints in Shibuya). The silhouette matters: our first rectangle-based attempt took out a street lamp standing next to a pedestrian, leaving its head floating in mid-air. Poles and guard rails survive on thin/elongated shape rules; individuals fused with barriers get finished off with manual masks after a visual check.

06 STEP 5: Stack the two layers and drive

Finally, assemble one USD: define a physicsScene on a Z-up stage, reference the 3DGS as-is, embed the collider as a Mesh with CollisionAPI (approximation=none), and call MakeInvisible() on it. That completes the two-layer environment — appearance from 3DGS, physics from the collider.

Vehicles drive on angular-velocity targets applied to the wheel joints via DriveAPI. For straight runs, stiffen the rear steering joints and add a simple lane-keeping controller that turns lateral offset and velocity into a left/right wheel-speed differential.

What each use case needsThese five steps make collision avoidance, path planning, and LiDAR/depth sensor testing production-usable. Camera-based learning additionally wants a sky dome to fill the black void and an environment wrapper (reset / observation / reward APIs). To suppress the remaining ride vibration, swap the vehicle for a PhysX Vehicle (omni.physx.vehicle) with sprung suspension.

07 Benchmark: relighting does NOT work (yet)

A 3DGS bakes in the light of its capture day. We tested whether post-processing can change the time of day, and record the negative result.

  • Scene lights do nothing. In Isaac Sim (ParticleField rendering), swinging a DistantLight from 8000 to 300 intensity and white to sunset colour changed the rendered pixels by no more than render noise (mean 2.7/255). 3DGS emits its own baked radiance; lights cannot illuminate it.
  • Shadow baking fails on quality. We ray-tested each gaussian toward the sun against the watertight collider and graded lit versus shadowed gaussians, at both 18° and 35° sun elevations. The new shadows stack on top of the shading already baked into the scan, reading as fading and dirt rather than evening light. Per-gaussian colour edits cannot avoid this double-shading in principle.
  • The only real route is inverse-rendering retraining along the lines of GS³, which cannot be bolted onto an existing scan. If you need time-of-day variants today, scan at multiple times of day.

08 Benchmark data and limits

The recipe was selected indoors (a meeting room), where ground truth is easy to measure. Healing takes the scanner mesh from 4.86% holes to 0%, with a median deviation from LiDAR of 1.1 cm, desk-leg occupancy of 29.7% (3DGS truth 27.2%; raw scanner mesh 18.6%), and 56/56 cubes at rest in the drop test. Drive a robot at a desk and it touches the edge and stops within ±5 mm — no clipping, no climbing.

What this meansA plain rule — do not delete or invent measured data — beat the learned priors. Niantic reached for a depth model because a 360° camera was all they had. Anyone holding measured LiDAR has a shorter, more certain route. The best answer to the same problem changes with the hardware in your hands.
Limits of this procedureThe numbers come from two scenes: the meeting room and Shibuya. No surface can be built where the LiDAR never looked, so keep spawns and routes inside the observed region when training. Outdoors adds two caveats. First, de-ghosting (STEP 4) removes the solids but not the shadow stains baked into the road colour, and road never observed under parked cars stays as dark holes. Second, with the LiDAR cloud merged into the reference, high billboards survive — but wispy low-opacity content in the truly unmeasured far field can still be deleted. Anything needing millimetre-accurate shape still deserves a CG model.

09 Summary

Turning a LiDAR-fusion 3DGS scan into an Isaac Sim environment fits in five lines.

  • STEP 1: align the frame to Z-up and pin the renderer to RaytracedLighting before loading.
  • STEP 2: reference = scanner mesh + raw LiDAR cloud, then banded deletion (0.74% in Shibuya). A mesh-only reference destroys real content.
  • STEP 3: collider = raw mesh + ground-fill patches only; wall holes stay raw; zero leaks over the drivable area.
  • STEP 4: snap the road onto the measured surface (bumps 6 cm → 1–2 cm) and de-ghost with silhouette masks + LiDAR pole protection (1.1% deleted in total).
  • STEP 5: stack the two layers in one USD, make the collider invisible, and drive a PhysX Vehicle with real suspension. No retraining anywhere.
