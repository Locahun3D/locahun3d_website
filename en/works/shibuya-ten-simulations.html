Ten Simulations Worth Running
in a 3DGS Shibuya Crossing

This is Nakamura from LOCAHUN 3D. Now that a scanned 3DGS city can carry a driving robot (last article), the question is what is worth simulating in the Shibuya scramble crossing. We ran ten candidates. The environment's strength is that appearance and shape of a dense real city are both measured, so we chose topics where visual complexity, the real placement of objects, and people flow decide the answer. For each, what it shows and what it does not.

Common setupSame two-layer environment as last time (untouched 3DGS for looks, raw scanner mesh for physics), Isaac's stock Nova Carter, Isaac Sim 6.0.1. Videos play at about twice simulation time. There is no human-labelled ground truth yet and no comparison with a real robot — every "what it shows" below is within that limit.

01 Can it cross before the light changes?

The first question any delivery robot gets. The answer here: the path was right, but the controller was too slow — against a green phase of a few tens of seconds (not yet measured on site), speed lost at turns and corrections mattered more than straight-line speed. Since controllers can be swapped in the same environment, the speed plan needed to make the crossing can be settled before deployment. Not shown: the real signal cycle, and slowdowns caused by other pedestrians (see 02).

02 Coexisting with moving pedestrians (can it stop?)

The scramble's "people from every direction" situation: can the robot stop and restart? The LiDAR picked up both the 3DGS city and the walkers, and the robot stopped and restarted for crossings. Not shown: real human behaviour (mutual avoidance, hesitation). Adding people-flow data and pedestrian agents is where this environment gains the most.

03 Where perception gets confused among the signs

Giant signage, reflective glass and painted road surfaces are visual noise you only get from a scan of the real place. Shown the same spot from many directions, the 2D model changed its mind on the paved plaza, thin posts and fences. What it shows: where your model is weak in this street, as a collection of failure cases. Not shown: accuracy — no human labels, so this is about instability, not correctness.

04 Reaching landmarks named in words

As a test bed for language-guided navigation, writing tasks with real landmarks is a big advantage. Here the mapping from words to map coordinates was done by hand, and the robot drove there autonomously; all four reached. Not shown: the language-understanding half (no language model connected), and only four tasks so far.

05 Evacuation flow and which exits matter

Baseline material for thinking about evacuation and robot-guided crowds. Because it runs over the real fences, police box and subway entrances, the pinch points come from the terrain. Not shown: people are a simple model (constant speed to the nearest exit, simplified pushing) with no calibration to real evacuation behaviour or counts — trends, not numbers.

06 Blind spots of a patrol route

The figure at the top. One patrol pass sees 85% of the surrounding ground; the remaining blind spots are behind the police box, in the shadow of poles and bollards, and in building recesses. Because it comes from the real layout, patrol routes and extra camera positions can be argued before going on site. Not shown: only one sensor height; no temporary occlusion by moving people and vehicles.

07 How sensor models differ in the same street

Swapping real-model presets gives material for the robot's sensor design. Not shown: reflectivity, rain and fog, intensity matching to real units — density and reach only. In our setup one LiDAR ran per environment, so each model needed the environment reopened.

08 Robustness to time of day (shadows)

For daytime time-of-day robustness this composite produces test images. Not possible: night, rain and lit neon (colours are baked). Use it for "shadows only".

09 Kerbs and steps

What we learned by trying: this crossing has almost no kerbs around its crosswalks (it is barrier-free). Nova Carter passed the road's small bumps at walking pace and stopped at the corner building's wall. Kerb-climbing needs a location that has kerbs — last article's vehicle did climb a pavement step, so per-robot comparison is possible if the spot is chosen.

10 Event set-up check

A non-robot use. Clashes between temporary structures and real ones, camera sightlines and free circulation can be checked in the full-scale street. It has the strongest synergy with our core location-scouting work; with the equipment dimensions, anything can be placed the same way.

11 Summary: usable now, and not yet

  • Usable today: 01 crossing time, 02 stop/restart, 04 landmark tasks, 06 blind spots, 07 sensor comparison, 10 set-up checks — all cases where the real layout decides the answer, giving material for decisions before going on site.
  • Trends only: 03 perception instability (no ground truth), 05 evacuation flow (simple model), 08 time of day (shadows only).
  • Pick the place: 09 kerbs (this crossing has none).
  • Common limits: no human ground truth, no comparison with real-robot logs, and pedestrians are a simple model. Those two are what to add next.
