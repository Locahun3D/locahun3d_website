Refining 3DGS with AI: image versus video input for camera orbits

A Shibuya test separating photographic still quality from geometric consistency in motion.

I’m Kou from Locahun3D. An earlier GPT edit of a Shibuya 3DGS render gave me the photographic look I wanted. Could that look carry into a moving shot? I started with still-image refinement, then tried Kling, Wan, Seedance and Aleph.

Contents

  1. Refine still images
  2. Animate with Kling
  3. Compare orbiting videos
  4. Set the look in an image

Each image and clip includes its generation cost, expressed in US dollars using Runway’s purchased-credit rate. Subscription, capture and production costs are separate.

Conversion basis: US$0.01 per credit

01 Refine still images

First, I gave Nano Banana and Seedream the same 3DGS image and asked for a sunny midday architectural photograph. Alongside light and materials, I wanted to see how much each would change the composition. The output aspect-ratio settings differed.

For this image, Nano Banana’s brighter materials stand out. Seedream keeps closer to the original framing, but I would want to lift the darker finish. Both redraw details in the signs and buildings. An attractive image and a faithful location are two different things to look for.

03 Compare orbiting videos

I also wanted to try a slight orbit, where the buildings are seen from a changing angle. I rendered a three-second, six-degree move in Full HD with Blender’s LCC2_DIRECT, using the captured 3DGS colour without added lights. This new shot is the starting point for the orbit tests.

Compare Wan and Seedance

Side by side, the daylight and materials in the Seedance-plus-image version stand out. The reference image, prompt and requested duration also differ here. I see this as a comparison of ways to build a shot, rather than a ranking of the models alone.

Does a video reference preserve the camera?

Video reference brought the motion closer to the source than a single image did. Still, pausing at the same moment reveals shifts in building positions and shapes. These tests supply rendered frames, not Blender camera data. For compositing, that gap between the planned camera and the generated shot matters.

Including the Aleph clip below, the generations shown here cost US$10.62 equivalent. Including an Edit attempt left out of the Full HD comparison brings that to US$12.27. Repeated views of the same output count only once; the earlier GPT image and tests outside this article are excluded.

04 Set the look in an image, then carry it into video

The approach I found promising was to establish the finish in Seedream, then supply that image alongside the original 3DGS video. A still gives a concrete reference for the light and materials I want. For the final test, I tried this combination with Aleph.

Seedance and Aleph: appearance and cost

The Seedream reference improved Seedance’s sunny finish and poster retention. That workflow cost US$3.83 equivalent for about four seconds, including the image. The Aleph shot below cost US$0.93 for three seconds. With different lengths and settings, I would weigh the changes to signs and windows alongside the price.

What matters when adding CG

A photographic finish does not remove camera or geometry shifts when it is time to add CG. Full HD describes the frame size, not how much detail it contains. Looking at the small lettering and window frames, I still want more clarity.

Lighting needs its own treatment. The shadows from capture are baked into the 3DGS colour, so adding a light does not simply change their direction. To add CG to these clips, I would start by matching the background’s sun and exposure, then work on contact and shadows. That work comes after the generation shown here.

Taking the Seedream image into Aleph

I registered the Seedream edit as the custom first frame in Aleph 2.0 Edit Studio and supplied the original 3DGS clip. Below is the resulting three-second Full HD video, without further colour correction or AI upscaling.

The daylight on the road and glass brings the original 3DGS closer to a photograph, and the large DMM poster survives. Looking closer, though, faces and small lettering have been redrawn, and the orbit still drifts from the source. There is promise in carrying a finished still into motion. For work that needs the actual location intact, preserving those details is the next problem to solve.

Aleph generation notes

The Aleph generation used existing credits worth US$0.84. I reused the Seedream image from the earlier comparison. Including its US$0.09 cost, this workflow totals US$0.93 equivalent.
