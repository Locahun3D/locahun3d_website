3DGS Scans Do Not Work
in Vectorworks

This is Kou Nakamura from Locahun 3D. Stage lighting and interior studios run on Vectorworks, so I wanted to know whether a 3D scan of a real location can join that workflow. I imported all three formats a scanner produces into Vectorworks Spotlight 2026 and tested each one. The short version: none of them are usable in practice. Here is what breaks in each case, measured, and what to do instead.

The conclusion, up frontVectorworks does not support the first format at all, the second can be measured but has no surfaces, and the third displays correctly but makes you wait on every view change. There is no compatible plugin for Vectorworks either. Rather than forcing scans into CAD, review and share site conditions in a dedicated viewer and keep drafting in CAD.

00 First — what are these three formats?

A single scanning session produces three different kinds of data. The names sound technical, but the ideas are simple.

  • 1. 3DGS (3D Gaussian Splatting) — a newer approach that fills space with countless tiny coloured particles to reproduce a photographic look. It captures atmosphere and material better than anything else, which makes it ideal for conveying what a place actually feels like.
  • 2. Point cloud — simply a dot placed at every measured position, each carrying its own colour. The coordinates are accurate, so this is what surveying uses.
  • 3. Mesh — the dots joined into triangular surfaces with photographs mapped onto them. This is the same format as a conventional 3D model, and because it has actual surfaces it should be the most CAD-friendly of the three.

Going in, the mesh was the one I expected to work. Here is what each of them actually did.

Test setupA city block captured with a handheld scanner (XGRIDS Lixel K2), imported into Vectorworks Spotlight 2026. The point cloud holds 126 million points; the mesh is split into three parts at 3.37 million polygons each. The same data was opened in Blender 4.5 — a widely used 3D application — for comparison.

01 1. 3DGS — Vectorworks does not support it

Vectorworks does not support 3DGS. A 3DGS file stores more than positions — each particle also carries what colour it is, how transparent it is and what shape it takes — but Vectorworks has no way to read any of that. On import it takes only the positions and discards everything else, colour included. What lands on screen is a sparse scatter of near-black dots.

Calculating the colour beforehand and converting the file to a conventional coloured format also works — the colour does come through. But at that point what you have is format 2, a point cloud, not 3DGS. The defining quality of 3DGS, where the surface changes as you move around it, is lost the moment you convert. Converting, in other words, is not "using 3DGS in Vectorworks".

No compatible plugin existsAs of August 2026 there is no add-on that lets Vectorworks display 3DGS. A request is open on the official user community but has not been implemented. A point-cloud product called PointCab 4Vectorworks exists, but it does not support 3DGS either.

02 2. Point cloud — you can measure it, but it has no surfaces

Vectorworks supports point clouds natively, so they import without any conversion. On colour fidelity alone, this is the best of the three.

But 16-bit colour comes out wrongThe file this scanner produced stores colour in 0–65535 steps (16-bit). Open that directly in Vectorworks and building walls come out painted in fluorescent green and bright red — nothing like the real thing. The colour data in the file is fine: genuinely green or red points account for only about 2% of the cloud. Convert the colour to 0–255 steps (8-bit) before importing and it displays correctly. The image below is after that conversion.

Points in the cloud accept snapping, so you can measure between two points and place dimension lines. If all you need is to pull dimensions off the existing site, that is enough.

But a point cloud is literally a collection of dots — there are no surfaces. You cannot click a wall to select it as a face, or extrude and cut against that face. Measuring, and tracing over it as an underlay, is where a point cloud stops; anything beyond that needs geometry modelled separately.

03 3. Mesh — it displays, but it is heavy to work in

A mesh has real surfaces, so you can click to select it inside Vectorworks and the photographic detail displays properly. It is the only one of the three that could serve as a base for drawing work.

The problem turned out to be speed. Opening the same data in Blender makes the gap obvious.

TaskVectorworks Spotlight 2026Blender 4.5
LoadingAbout 20–30 secondsTens of seconds
Switching viewAbout 1.5 seconds of waitingNo wait
Other operations0.3–2 secondsFully interactive
Photographic detailDisplays correctlyDisplays without issue

Waiting 1.5 seconds on every view change is genuinely tiresome when drafting means changing the view constantly. But it is not so heavy that it cannot be opened. And these figures cover one of the three parts the block was split into — loading the whole scan roughly triples the load.

04 So what should you do?

Having tried all three, my conclusion is that it is faster to stop trying to put scan data into CAD at all.

  • To review and share site conditions — use a viewer built for 3DGS. No conversion, no thinning the data down, and the real texture of the location stays intact. It runs in a browser, so nobody else needs to buy software.
  • To draft and design — stay in Vectorworks. This is what CAD is genuinely good at.
  • To connect the two — measure only the dimensions you actually need in the viewer, and carry those numbers into CAD. There is no reason to hand an enormous dataset to a drafting tool.

Vectorworks and Locahun 3D are not competitors. They are good at different things, and running them side by side turns out to be the fastest route — that is what this round of testing made clear.
